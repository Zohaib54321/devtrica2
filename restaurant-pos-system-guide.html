Restaurant POS Guide: Where the Money Actually Leaks

A restaurant can serve a full house every evening and still end the month thin. The reason is almost never the menu price. It is the distance between what was ordered, what was cooked, what was billed and what was taken out of the store — four numbers that should match and usually do not.

The four places money leaks in a restaurant

Orders that never became bills

A table orders, the kitchen cooks, the food goes out, and the bill is either forgotten in the rush or quietly never raised. With paper order pads there is no way to catch this, because the only record of the order left with the food.

Staff meals and complimentary items counted as nothing

Every restaurant feeds its staff and comps the occasional dessert. That is fine — it is a cost, not a crime. The problem is when it is invisible. Ingredients leave the store, no sale is recorded, and your food cost percentage silently rises with nobody able to explain why.

Portion drift

A recipe says 180 grams of chicken. Over a busy month, the actual average creeps to 220. Nothing is stolen and nobody is careless, but that is roughly 20% more cost on your highest-volume dish. Without recipe-level tracking, this never surfaces.

Wastage that goes in the bin unrecorded

Spoilage, burnt dishes, returns from unhappy customers, prep that was not used. Each one is small. Together they are often the difference between a good month and an average one — and they only become manageable once they are counted.

KOT: the single biggest control you can add

Kitchen Order Ticket is the mechanism that closes the first leak. The waiter enters the order on the POS, and it prints in the kitchen or appears on a kitchen screen. Nothing is cooked without a ticket, and every ticket exists in the system.

What that gives you immediately:

  • Every dish that left the kitchen is attached to a table and a bill.
  • Comps and staff meals must be entered as such, so they show up as a recorded cost instead of vanishing.
  • Cancellations and voids require a reason and leave a record of who authorised them.
  • The kitchen gets legible tickets instead of handwriting, which cuts remakes.
  • You can see how long orders take between entry and completion, which is the actual measure of kitchen speed.

If you implement only one thing from this article, make it this. The discipline of "no ticket, no food" fixes more than any report ever will.

Table and order management

Beyond billing, the floor needs practical handling:

  • Live table status — open, occupied, ordered, billed — so nobody walks the floor to check.
  • Merge and transfer for groups that join or move.
  • Running bills so items can be added across a long sitting.
  • Split bills by item or by person, which is normal for groups and a nightmare on paper.
  • Order types — dine-in, takeaway and delivery kept separate, because their costs and margins differ and you need them reported separately.
  • Modifiers — extra cheese, no onions, spice level — attached to the item so the kitchen gets them and the price adjusts.

Recipe-level inventory and food cost

This is what separates a billing machine from a restaurant management system. Instead of tracking "chicken karahi" as a product, the system knows a chicken karahi consumes specific quantities of chicken, tomatoes, oil, spices and gas.

Once recipes are defined, three things become possible:

  1. Automatic stock deduction. Sell forty karahis and the store is reduced by the ingredients for forty karahis. No manual stock entry.
  2. Theoretical versus actual variance. At month end, compare what the recipes say should have been consumed against what actually left the store. The gap is your wastage, portion drift and shrinkage, in one number you can finally manage.
  3. Real per-dish margin. You learn which items earn and which are popular but barely break even. That changes how you price and how you design the menu.

Setting up recipes takes effort — usually a few days with the chef — and it is the highest-return work in the whole implementation. Start with your top twenty selling items rather than the whole menu.

The five reports worth checking

Most systems produce dozens of reports. These five are the ones that change decisions:

  • Item-wise sales with margin. Not just what sold most — what earned most. The two lists are rarely the same.
  • Hourly sales. Tells you where to put staff and when a deal actually makes sense.
  • Order type split. Dine-in versus takeaway versus delivery, with costs attached. Delivery volume can look excellent and still be your worst margin.
  • Variance report. Theoretical consumption versus actual. The single most useful number in a restaurant.
  • Void and discount report. By user. Not because staff are dishonest, but because patterns here reveal process problems quickly.

What to look for when choosing

  1. Does it run offline? A restaurant POS that stops when the internet drops is not viable during dinner service.
  2. Can your staff learn it in one shift? Turnover in restaurants is high. Complexity is a permanent training cost.
  3. Can you edit the menu yourself? Prices, deals and availability change constantly. Calling the vendor each time is not workable.
  4. Does it handle shifts and cash closing? Opening cash, closing cash, variance by cashier.
  5. Will it handle a second branch? Ask now, not after you sign the lease.

Frequently asked questions

What is a KOT in a restaurant POS?

KOT stands for Kitchen Order Ticket. When a waiter enters an order, it prints in the kitchen or shows on a kitchen screen. It ensures nothing is cooked without a recorded order, which is the main control against unbilled food.

How does a restaurant POS reduce wastage?

By tracking recipes. The system knows what ingredients each dish consumes, so it can compare theoretical consumption against actual stock used. The gap exposes wastage, over-portioning and shrinkage that daily sales reports never show.

Can a restaurant POS work without internet?

It should. A well-built system runs locally so service continues when the connection drops, then syncs to the cloud afterwards. Confirm this before buying — it is the most common weak point in cloud-only systems.

Do I need recipe setup for a small cafe?

It pays off even at small scale, but start with your top twenty selling items rather than the full menu. Those usually account for most of your volume and therefore most of your food cost.

Can the POS handle dine-in, takeaway and delivery together?

Yes, and it should keep them separate in reporting. Their margins differ significantly, especially once delivery commissions and packaging are counted, so combined figures hide the real picture.
